XRF Calibration at Birla Carbon

Material Analyst Intern. Marietta, GA. January to April 2025.

RoleMaterial Analyst Intern
ToolXRF spectrometer
GoalMeasure sulfur in oil
ResultAccurate calibration and a method the company can reuse

The Problem

Birla Carbon makes carbon black from oil. The amount of sulfur in that oil matters for environmental reasons, so the company needs a reliable way to measure it. They had an X-ray fluorescence (XRF) spectrometer that could do the job. My task was to calibrate it so it could measure sulfur in oil accurately. I also checked its accuracy against a combustion based method.

The Process

An XRF machine hits a sample with X-rays. Each element in the sample gives off X-rays at its own energy. The stronger the sulfur signal, the more sulfur is in the sample. But the machine needs a calibration curve to turn that signal into a real number.

To build the curve, I measured oils with known sulfur content. I plotted what the machine read against the known value for each one. If everything is right, the points fall on a clean line that the machine can use for any new sample.

The Challenge

My curve did not come out clean. It had mistakes, and at times it was not very accurate. I tried over and over, and I stayed stuck for a while.

What finally worked was taking a step back. Instead of trying again, I tested the curve itself. I measured each known oil against its own calibration curve. A good curve should give back each oil's labeled value. Most came back close. The oil with the smallest amount of sulfur had the worst results by far.

There were two likely reasons. XRF can struggle with very small amounts of an element, and that sample had very little sulfur. Its label could also have been slightly wrong. Either way, it did not belong in the curve. I removed it and rebuilt the calibration, and it worked great afterwards.

The Outcome

The final calibration measured sulfur in oil accurately. I then designed and documented a method the company can use to measure sulfur with the XRF going forward. It includes the calibration procedure and a repeatable way to collect data for future analysis.

What I Learned

Repeating the same steps harder does not fix a hidden problem. Stepping back and checking my assumptions did, even the ones I thought were known for certain. I also learned how to write a procedure clear enough for someone else to follow after I left.
